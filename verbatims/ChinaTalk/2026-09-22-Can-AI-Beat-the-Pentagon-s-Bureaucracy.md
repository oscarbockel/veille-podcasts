## The U-2 Parable: Innovation Beyond the Technology

I'll tell the quick cliff notes version of the story of the U-2. It was built in a classified program with government buyers, government money giving money to industry, and basically unleashing a whole bunch of innovation around sensors and high altitude. They built everything new—all the pieces of this stitched together—incredibly innovative and done very quickly in a secret lab. 

If you fast-forward to the Cuban Missile Crisis, suddenly we have overflight imagery of missile sites in Cuba. We're getting really good raw intelligence in a way that the Russians didn't know we were getting, and it was kind of incredible and miraculous to US decision-makers: they're actually seeing changes on the ground over time. The plane, by the way—I was looking at stuff recently—is still in service, which is actually incredible. It was that revolutionary and remains really valuable still; they keep talking about decommissioning it and they decide not to. 

The technology, of course, is incredibly important, as was the innovation to put it all together. But **the reason I like this metaphor, and why I think it's relevant for today while we talk about AI, is it's not just the technology, not just the plane: it's that the way they bought it was important.** The innovations around acquisition reform, the way they acquired it, the way industry and government worked together to do that—and then **the way that the information coming out of this plane was actually analyzed and institutionalized through intelligence analysis**. 

They built a whole new intelligence service, the NPIC [National Photographic Interpretation Center], which eventually turned into NGA. How they built that whole chain of judgment and then, probably most importantly, put it in front of the president and other decision-makers—that is the miraculous piece of the story. 

Now, pivoting to the AI moment, **we're in this moment where the technology has moved so much faster because so much of it is commercial—not managed in a government program, but coming from industry. It has moved so far ahead of our ability to integrate it into the actual systems, into the workflows, into the decision-making processes, that we are now kind of flat-footed.** That's a lot of what I work on at my company and what I worked on when I was in government: trying to think through those other pieces that need to change, not just the AI. **That's why APIs alone are not enough.**

Let's spend a little more time on the U-2. What were the technological unlocks that you needed to take that photo and realize that the missiles were coming into Cuba? You needed new lenses, a new canister, this high-altitude plane...

High-altitude flight, right. At the time, no one had ever flown that high, so even just the equipment on the pilots was new. Could you even see it from the ground? No, because Eisenhower was doing it for a while over the Soviet Union before they even realized. 

The other piece about it that's so incredible to remember is pace and speed. Doing things with pace and speed in an engineering way is actually very hard. We're much more used to: we do a first test, then we do a second test, then we collect data, then we go back and refine it. I'm not saying that's the wrong way to do it—that is how you do engineering, especially if you're going to put pilots into life-threatening environments. But **the ability to move more quickly through that and to take more risk is part of the magic of that technology.** 

Back to now, I think that's why **we are seeing such a focus on pushing experimentation as far as we can, with senior leaders saying, "I'm willing to accept some risk here because the mission is so important, and we need to take a little bit more risk to try these technologies out in more operational environments."**

Let's go from "Okay, great, we have this photo, which is an incredible thing that no one really knows how to deal with" to: what was the entire intelligence apparatus?

They take these canisters out of the cameras, blow them up into huge printouts on blotted paper, and lay them out on this floor in the NPIC in a classified space. Literally, people are taking manuals or intelligence reports from Soviet weapons intelligence analysis, and they're looking with magnifying glasses: "Is that the right paint on the side of this truck that means it's a missile launcher? Is the shape of the missile consistent with what our intelligence report says?" 

You're taking the raw intelligence and analyzing it with human judgment, with experts on military equipment, blending that all together into an assessment, and doing it very fast. If you follow the Cuban Missile Crisis, you can go day-by-day in these really difficult NSC Situation Room decisions: "Do we attack? Do we put up a blockade? What do we message back to the Soviets?" These decisions were happening very quickly, and you didn't have a lot of time to sit back and do a deeper memo analysis.

## The Information Overhang and Core Business Systems

Coming from then, where it was clear you had a few new pieces of data and photos and were going to do as much as you could with them, now we're in almost the opposite: this crazy world where there is almost too much information. It's not like you can build the NPIC at the same time as designing the exact spy plane you want to have the NPIC for. You just have this giant technological overhang which is growing in directions that you're not telling it to, and you're not in control. How is it going? How are people processing this?

You want to give people their due for pushing. In this current team in the Pentagon and other places—and I spent time in both the State Department, the NSC, and the Department of Defense in the last five years or so—people are working really hard at it. But look, there's a lot to do. 

You're seeing pockets of a lot of success, more in operational spaces where they're really making progress. You see this in the news around **CJADC2 [Combined Joint All-Domain Command and Control]**. Giving people situational awareness of the entire battlefield, using AI to identify objects and intel, and bringing it live and quickly to decision-makers in the field—you're really seeing a lot of progress there. 

**Where we're seeing less progress, candidly, is in what I'd call core business systems.** Areas that may be seen historically as quote "business systems." My personal belief is that **I don't think of systems as mission systems versus business systems. Increasingly, everything is a mission system.** 

All of the agencies in the federal government are really starting to realize that **if they want operational systems out at the edge supporting warfighters or decision-makers, they need to have really, really good data from their "business systems"—logistics, personnel, finance. Those are really warfighting functions too.** You have to invest in getting the data cleaned, organized, and ready for use at an operational tempo. A lot of these business systems were not built to operate at an operational tempo; they haven't had to be before. 

A very simple example: in some of these business systems, literally the rules say you only have to update the status once a week on some form. Well, that's a policy. Someone has written a policy that says supply sergeants in the Army—of which there are probably 10,000 or 20,000; I was in logistics in the Army, so imagine the challenge of going to your supply sergeant and saying, "Hey, you actually have to input this into this system every day instead of once a week, because some decision-maker wants that data every day." That is really hard; that is a big boat to turn around. The value has to be there. And then, of course, a lot of times we're giving them systems that are really not easy to use. We're making it hard for them to put the data in, and now we're telling them the tempo and latency of that data needs to be a lot faster.

## Incentivizing Bureaucracies for Radical Change

When you were sitting there in the 1950s imagining a U-2 plane, the vision of what you wanted it to accomplish was a relatively straightforward thing to imagine: "We're just going to have cameras in the sky and be able to take photos of stuff when we want to." When you're talking about modernizing mission systems and logistics systems, it is not as obvious what an agentic warfare bureaucracy looks like. Do you have a pitch you give people? Are we just feeling our way into it?

It's a bit of an unfair comparison, I will give you that much, because the U-2 was doing one thing. Its purpose was to do one thing, whereas **AI can do almost anything that is a piece of knowledge work within the confines of these models, and the border of what is possible is changing every darn day.** 

It's a general-purpose technology. What I'd say is: every command, every office in government knows their problems. They know the things that drive their people crazy, that cost them money and time. Half the time I meet with folks in government about these things, they ask me, "Hey, what do you think I should do?" And I'm like, "No, you tell me. You know your problems; I'll help try to take these problems out of your brain." You just have to have a feel for what your organization's biggest problems are. You have to be able to follow the current of what will solve people's problems, what you can get money for (which is always a skill in government too), and what will deliver wins for your leadership. You try to find the middle ground of the Venn diagram there, and that's kind of where you start. Once you get some traction, you can then move on to other challenges.

If you have a company that's not going to do this, they'll just get outcompeted. But the checkpoints you have on whether the Air Force is being outcompeted or national security is safe—we get them hopefully once or twice a century, hopefully less than that. The lack of forcing functions just means that you can not be good for longer.

I think that's right. I think, though, we underestimate our ability to add our own mechanisms to drive that. This is where leadership and personnel policy come into account: **we need better mechanisms—both carrots and sticks—to encourage risk-taking and innovators in and out of uniform.** People follow incentives. People are ambitious and want to do a good job. **If you have a system that encourages this type of behavior, people taking big risks are rewarded, while people who are not moving fast enough face career stifling or removal.** That can feel crude, but that is the mechanism you have to follow: clear rewards and downsides for people based on whether they're making progress or not.

The problem of the "Garrett 1.0" version of just solving the problems everyone can identify is that you're only identifying problems that also work for you. No one is ever going to tell you—or it is rare to find a person who will tell you—"My job shouldn't exist."

People will say, "I really don't like the way I have to do my job, though." There are so many people who feel so stifled in the way they're working; they would like to have better tools and a better process. It won't be 100% of the workforce; there are definitely going to be people who are just used to how they work. But that's an adoption curve that any technology faces: early adopters, then people who jump on the bandwagon once it works, and there will always be people who would rather do it the old way. You just kind of have to force it. 

There's a Jen Pahlka anecdote of her talking to some general about how to fix the system, saying, "I don't even know if we should cut your budget, this stuff is really important." And the general responded, **"No, 50%. We need to cut 50%, because that's the only way to get people to change things."** We're not going to have someone selling software to the government come and say we need to cut stuff by 50%, but programs have to have winners and losers.

Look, as an industry, we want money spent on the most valuable things for the government, even if that's not going to us. We care about the government running well. Making change in what you're buying oftentimes is not a bad thing, because we are excited to tackle new problems. A lot of times, programs that are growing are more exciting for our people, and they want to jump on something new. Change happens in this industry, and it will happen in every industry, but it's not all bad.

## Software, Second-Mover Advantage, and the Edge

This is going to radically remake how organizations can be structured. That radical destruction is going to start happening much more on the software side. Managing the logistics supply chain in the warehouses isn't going anywhere; if anything, we just want it more efficient, or you might end up having to do more of it. Is the answer through Congress, or is it crazy to think: "Look, we have the most powerful military in the world, maybe let's not rock the boat"?

The reason we have the most powerful military in the world is because it can adapt and change, and it needs to. That has been true forever. I think we're just facing a very radical pace of change at the current moment, so we need leaders who are willing to move and change at that pace.
