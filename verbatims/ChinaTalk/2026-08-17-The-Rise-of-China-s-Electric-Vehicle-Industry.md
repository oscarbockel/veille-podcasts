## The EV Puzzle: Private Dynamism vs. State Capitalism

How did China grow its EV industry? Hint: **it's not really central planning from Beijing. It was a magical concoction of entrepreneurs and local officials using their *guanxi* to dance around giant SOEs and central government regulation, leverage private and foreign capital, and just be really excellent business people**, which has led to our situation in 2024 of these world-beating companies which have a ton of overcapacity and are set to take over the planet. 

To discuss, we have Professor Fengming Lu, who recently wrote a fantastic paper about this topic, which we'll link to in the show notes, as well as Irene Zhang, contributor and analyst at ChinaTalk. We also get into the lessons the story holds for other strategic industries like robotics and AI, as well as a little discourse into the Chinese podcasting ecosystem. Professor Lu, welcome to the show.

"Well, it's really my honor to join ChinaTalk."

I had an amazing time reading the paper and thought it was just incredibly interesting to learn the long history of how the global wave of Chinese EVs we're seeing today really came about. I wanted to start by hearing the story on your side of how you and your co-author came up with the idea for this paper. How did you get interested in EVs as a research topic and decide to write this?

"Actually, it's related to my own research agenda. I started doing research about EVs a few years ago, roughly during the final years of COVID. After that, I was mostly doing fieldwork and started writing papers and working on book projects starting from last year. 

My co-author and I knew each other very well, and we came across several questions from senior scholars studying the Chinese government and the Chinese economy. One of the first puzzles they raised was that **in the last ten or twenty years, especially after 2012, you see this rise of SOEs (state-owned enterprises) in many corners of the Chinese economy, but the automotive sector—the EV sector—actually is an exception.** It's probably not the only exception if you look at tech sectors in China, which are mostly dominated by private firms rather than SOEs, but it's a pretty interesting puzzle. 

My co-author and I thought maybe it's a good idea to start writing a paper about this. My co-author also added cases from his expertise, like pharmaceuticals and steel manufacturing, showing that **the private sector, based on collaboration with Chinese local governments, actually excelled pretty much at the expense of traditional, more established SOEs.**"

I thought this was the most fascinating arc over the course of your paper. On the one hand, we have **Xi Jinping, the biggest SOE cheerleader we've had in a few administrations, but perhaps the most globally impressive, successful industrial leapfrogging has taken place in an industry where the SOEs were basically stuck and doing nothing.** 

There were two different development pathways that had been tried: partnering with foreign automakers, as well as building things up themselves. They had been fine, but not anything all that impressive. All of a sudden, as you explore in this paper, you have this really interesting mix of private capital, entrepreneurs, local governments, and regional incentives dancing around what space the central government and the big SOE carmakers left, creating this incredible flourishing we've seen over the past ten years. 

## The 1990s Landscape: "Three Bigs and Three Smalls"

Maybe let's do a timeline of the Chinese car industry. Why don't you take us back to the 1990s? Where can we start to see the seeds of the EV boom in that time?

"Back in the 90s, the Chinese automotive industry was in a very different shape compared to what it is now. The size of the market, if you're talking about passenger vehicles—cars, SUVs, and so on—was **only about half a million sold every year. It was a much smaller market, comparable to the size of Poland back then or Vietnam today.** 

For the Chinese government, their main concern was that, largely as a legacy of the Maoist era and economic decentralization in the 1980s, the Chinese automotive industry was super fragmented. Every province had their own carmaker except for Tibet; 29 or 30 others had their own. In the mid-1990s, someone did the count: **China had about 130 carmakers across the country**—roughly the same number as during the peak of the EV boom—only making a little bit more than one million vehicles total, including trucks and so on."

What I thought was fascinating, Fengming, is that it actually made sense to have a local manufacturer in whatever corner of your province because you had these Maoist controls on who gets which automobiles. If you wanted to have any cars in your region, you kind of had to make them yourself. Even though on its face it seems absurd to have a factory that makes 500 cars a year, that may actually have been economically productive if the difference was having 500 cars or trucks versus no cars or trucks.

"To some extent, it was pretty similar to the early British automotive sector, with countless brands and carmakers. What the Chinese government was concerned about came from the shock starting in the late 1970s and early 1980s when they started negotiating with European carmakers, especially Volkswagen. **Volkswagen and many others told them that if you want to make a carmaker efficient, you need a minimum scale of economy of at least 150,000 cars manufactured every year.** 

The Chinese regulators and policymakers bore in mind this magic number: 150,000 cars every year. They did some simple math in the 1980s: if the size of the market is well below one million, **it can only contain about four to six large-scale carmakers.** 

That is why, starting from the late 1980s, the Chinese government came up with the very famous automotive concept of the **'Three Bigs and Three Smalls'** (*San Da San Xiao*). Back in the 1990s, these were the legitimate carmakers you could see on roads in China. Among the big ones:
- **FAW** (First Automobile Works), which had a joint venture with Volkswagen;
- **Dongfeng** (the Second Automobile Works), which had a joint venture with Citroën;
- **Shanghai Automotive** (SAIC), which already had their joint venture with Volkswagen.

And the smaller ones were:
- **Beijing Jeep**;
- **Guangzhou Peugeot**;
- **Tianjin Xiali**, which had a license from Daihatsu making smaller cars from Japan.

Later on, some carmakers with defense contractor or arms-making backgrounds, of course state-owned, also snuck into this game, like Chang'an. But it was still a very restrictive list. Many of those carmakers pulled strings to get into those positions through elite politics. 

What you see in the 1990s is that all of those were approved carmakers. But you still had a bunch of other provinces and cities that wanted to get into the game. Back then, China had not yet joined the WTO, so tariffs were very high. **In the beginning of the 1990s, there were roughly 150% to 200% tariffs on imported cars**, and later on that was lowered to around 80% to 120%. For many local governments and firms, it was immensely profitable to make cars—or, an easier way, to smuggle cars, especially in places like Guangdong, Fujian, and interestingly Shandong, Liaoning, and Jilin, because they were close to South Korea.

Other cities were trying to figure out their own ways of getting into automotive manufacturing. Some of the giant carmakers in China today have their roots in the 1990s, starting out with very humble backgrounds, later created by or collaborating with local governments: companies like **Geely**, which owns Volvo Cars and brands like Zeekr, and **Chery**. Chery is rising so quickly in the European, British, and Australian markets, but people outside China—even outside Anhui province—know so little about it."

## The Rise of Chery: Rebellion, Welsh Engines, and Guerrilla Auto Making

Why don't you tell the origin story of how Chery grew starting in the 90s?

"It's a very interesting company. Originally, it was a state-owned enterprise in the 90s and all the way into the 2000s, but it was not owned by any large city, large province, or the central government. **It was an SOE of a city called Wuhu.** Very few people outside China, or even inside China, have heard of Wuhu. It's a city in the middle of Anhui province, about 500 to 600 kilometers—or 400 miles—away from Shanghai.

In the early 1990s, Wuhu and Anhui province were not in great shape. Anhui had been lagging behind its neighbors like Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Shanghai since the start of the reform era. They were looking for a new industry that could boost their economy. Based on in-depth fieldwork and materials I collected, I reconstructed the story. 

In the early 1990s, Wuhu leaders were looking for a new growth engine. In a village near the city, there was a Township and Village Enterprise (TVE). They had started collaborating with an arms maker in Henan province, got connections, found engineers, and started building cars based on kit parts from South Korea, manufacturing on a very small scale of about 3,000 to 5,000 cars a year."

How do Township and Village Enterprises end up turning into small car manufacturers?

"TVEs were flourishing in China in the 1980s. But starting from the late 80s and early 1990s, some of them were hit hard because their products were not technologically advanced, and **after 1989, there was a harsh crackdown on TVEs from the central government.** A lot of TVEs were looking for a new way out. 

This particular TVE found a new way: making cars. It was immensely successful because, while their cars were not of very good quality, **they were sold at around $10,000—roughly 70,000 to 80,000 RMB. That was much cheaper than products offered by the 'Three Bigs and Three Smalls' SOEs.** They made quite a lot of money and became the first company in Wuhu to exceed 100 million RMB in revenue.

Officials in the Wuhu government looked into why they made so much money, and it turned out to be car manufacturing. The leaders of Wuhu discussed this with leaders of Anhui province, saying, 'Maybe we should have a try at making cars.' The quote from one provincial official was, 'The Party and the government can do something in car making.'

Initially, they pulled strings with FAW, the 'First Automobile Works.' The general manager of FAW back then was a man named **Geng Zhaojie**, still a very respected figure in the Chinese auto industry. He happened to be from the area near Wuhu. The leader of Wuhu befriended him at the National People's Congress—China's rubber-stamp legislature, which may not be meaningful as a legislature, but is very meaningful as a social club. 

They befriended each other, partnered with an entrepreneur nearby, and thought about establishing a carmaker in Wuhu. But it didn't end well. Government officials in Wuhu told me that **FAW was extremely arrogant toward Wuhu. FAW viewed itself as the 'eldest son' of the Chinese automotive industry.** Administratively, FAW was more senior than the city of Wuhu and had been the cradle of so many political leaders in China. They felt they didn't need to listen to the Wuhu government. All FAW wanted to do was make peripheral components, like truck chassis, in Wuhu. 

Officials in Wuhu told me that managers sent by FAW, whenever they wanted to discuss anything, would smash into the conference room in the middle of meetings, shouting, 'Is your Party Secretary here? I want to talk to him.' It was very rude, and eventually Wuhu couldn't stand it.

At that time, Wuhu had a windfall. **They sold a local cement plant to a nearby company for 200 million RMB—a massive amount of money back then.** That cement business later became **Hailuo Shuini (Conch Cement)**, which became one of the largest cement companies in China and a Fortune 500 company. 

Wuhu heard that there was an engine manufacturing assembly line on sale in Britain, in Wales. They decided to organize a trip to Wales and **used that 200 million RMB, along with bank loans provided by the provincial government, to buy that engine line.** What they had learned from FAW was that if you don't have your own engine, you cannot make your own cars. They recruited engineers from FAW and elsewhere, and started their own carmaker: Chery.

Their first product was **reverse-engineered from the SEAT Toledo**, the Spanish version of the Volkswagen Jetta. The Jetta was one of the very few affordable family cars in China in the 1990s. Chery decided to go head-on with the Jetta, especially since they had recruited FAW engineers who knew how to build that kind of car.

In the 2000s and 2010s, Chery gradually became more of a private firm by introducing a management buyout (MBO), and recently filed for an IPO in Hong Kong. Today, the governments of Anhui and Wuhu still hold about 30% of the stock, but are no longer the majority shareholder.

Chery is a prime example of local governments dancing around restrictions. **Wuhu was blocked from making cars because they didn't have a production license from the central government.** For the first few years, lacking a national license, **they were officially only allowed to sell their cars within Anhui province.** 

Knowing that the Anhui market alone could never sustain an automaker, they tried crazy workarounds. When I was a kid—about ten years old, already a car fan—**Chery sold cars in Chengdu, an emerging market, with Anhui license plates.** If you were in Chengdu and wanted to buy one of the first batches of Chery cars, you had to take it with Anhui plates! The Chengdu Police Bureau couldn't stand this and raised complaints, telling buyers they had to register locally."
